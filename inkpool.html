On the English language

First, invest in a good dictionary or maybe even two. Buy the single-volume OED, Merriam- Webster Collegiate, or the latest edition of the American Heritage Dictionary of the English Language. Get them in print. The Learner’s editions of these books are good, too.

Next turn to Sites & Blogs: Purdue Online Writing Lab for grammar (with exercises) at Click me. Excellent resource.

Jack Lynch’s Guide to Grammar and Style at Click me. A more traditional and prescriptive guide with articles on usage and style.

America Heritage Dictionary at Click me . Not only to look up words, but for its dedicated blog as well, especially, for those working with US English.

Oxford English Dictionary at . This particular link takes you to their handy grammar section. Explore the rest.

The American Copy Editor’s Society has an excellent resources section at Click me

Arrant Pedantry at Click me . Linguist who writes about usage, grammar, and editing.

Oliver Kamm. Leader writer and columnist for The Times—read him at that paper.

Lingua Franca at Click me. Different authorities speak on language, grammar et al. Quite accessible.

Motivated Grammar at . A mathematician goes at grammar myths and descriptivism with logic and loads of write attitude.

Sentence First at Click me is a blog about the English Language from Ireland

And then there are usage guides. Yes, English is a vast sea to navigate through and these will help.

The Cambridge Guide to English by Pam Peters is excellent, but surprisingly little heard of. It mentions Australian and international English usage—which is a bonus—and draws recommendations from a corpus of usage.

The Chicago Guide to Grammar, Usage, and Punctuation by Bryan A. Garner, the pre-eminent authority on English in the US, is perhaps the finest survey in the descriptive tradition.

The Chicago Manual of Style is the grand daddy of the publishing world. Compendious and with a religious following.

Also, get Oliver Kamm’s book, Accidence will happen: The non-pedantic guide to English usage.

Grammar books abound – but which will really sustain our interest, turn out to be authoritative, and remain up-to-date with modern grammatical and linguistic contexts. Here is a short list:

For introductory works, consider Betty S. Azar’s series on grammar in the US and Raymond Murphy in the UK. These are justifiably reputed.

Understanding Grammar by Paul Roberts. An old book, with a historical approach, but extremely good.

The Grammar Book: An ESL/EFL Teacher’s Course by Marianne Celce-Murcia and Diane Larsen-Freeman is the odd choice, It is, however, excellent for a deeper appreciation.

Longman Student Grammar of Spoken and Written English by Douglas Biber, Susan Conrad and Jeffrey Leach is a good reference grammar. It is accessible at Click me

A Student’s Introduction to English Grammar by Rodney Huddleston and Geoffrey Pullum is the abridged version of their Cambridge grammar. A difficult work; its title is deceptive.
