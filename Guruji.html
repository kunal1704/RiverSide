You have been the sky to my poor tree While countless skies have languished as leaves And oceans of leaves have murmured lullabies before nothing

Still you alone have remained

Bring down rain forests of blessings Stir the landscape of your bliss

Now may these green songs touch The twin flowers of your feet

“Two birds with beautiful plumage live on the same tree together, in fast friendship. Of these, one eats the fruits of the tree; the other does not and keeps looking on. So in this body (the tree) there are two birds, with the same form and beautiful wings. One is the human soul eating the fruits of karma; the other is God Himself, of the same nature. He is also in this body, the Soul of our soul. He eats neither good nor evil fruits, but stands and looks on. He is the Guru.”

To the Guru who pours his mercy On the parched land of the soul Divested from reality— I take the refuge of His holy feet

To the Guru who wakes up Knowledge from its sleep, Turning its eyes inward— I take the refuge of His holy feet

To the Guru who flows as reality Into a waiting heart and a mind empty Of all conceptions and flux— I take the refuge of His holy feet

To the Guru who blows the conch of truth With ardent yet silent love Distressing the disciple from his sleep— I take the refuge of His holy feet

To the Guru who glancing at the disciple Wakes him to see The invisible face of his own self— I take the refuge of His holy feet

To the Guru who clasps the disciple To his own illimitable being Till he becomes one with it— I take the refuge of his holy feet

To the guru who plucks out the thorn of ‘I’ From his disciple’s long-suffering feet Uprooting the cause of sorrow

I gift the ‘I’— In final and full prostration forever

Rustling Leaves

The Guru is the destiny of the disciple. Under the sun of his grace, and the occasional fulsome baking, the disciple matures. He loses his pretensions and his prejudices, his false image of the self and realises; that realization is reflected through his human personality. Spirituality is a weaning away from false ideas rather than a gaining of something new. To grow spiritually is to realize that you are losing your outer sheaths and coming, at rates of speed fit for different temperaments, to your real identity. Spirituality is innate and never an object of acquisition, even if it be a mental state.

The significance of the Guru, under this viewpoint, is analogous to that of the gardener. An excellent gardener who can find seeds and manure and just the right sun and shade no matter what the soil type. The gardener spends some time gently digging at the hard soil—because that is what most of us are like: Hardened materialists with small stony hearts. With the spade of love, he gently breaks these into soft lumps without unsettling the top soil of our personality. Demolition of the personality is left for those who can take it. Had the Guru-gardener broken into our hearts with the full force of pure love, the personality would disintegrate. For most, that would be unbearable; a psychical rollercoaster that could not be ridden.

After tilling the ground, the Guru selects the seed that can be dropped into the flower bed. He pats disciplinary mud around it. Take this to mean sadhana: It can either be introduced with formality or without orthodoxy, with a casual command. But the vital energy that inspires the sadhana is the Guru’s grace.

The Guru then waters the seed and steps away for a while. The life of outer circumstances of events, of sorrow and happiness, shines on the young plant. It yearns for true light, and the Guru again steps in. Some weeding is required after a bit of experience gathering; maybe a strong pesticide; more manure or some slight tilling. He does what is needed and steps away again. The process is repeated. The disciple is tended and grown until he blooms into himself. The Guru keeps doing this for those earnest in his sanctuary. He sees into the nature of all those who come and understands who is ripe for what. By deep insight he knows of the shraddha, the commitment and conviction the disciple has made ever since he has been an individual being, and plants the seed natural to that environment. He does not make everybody into one kind of flower. The Guru is not cloning. He is growing a garden full of all kinds of flowers.

The Guru’s role is akin to the mother’s at home. She feeds, nurtures, cares, protects, teaches, grooms, prods, punishes, loves, forgives, loves again. The Guru is nothing else but the lap of the divine mother. To write about his significance is a foolish fancy. It is foolish because the plenitude of his nature and its transcendence can’t be described. Thus, whatever is wrought by words can only point to the Guru. It cannot describe him fully. It is fancy because the mediator in the act of writing is the mind. The mind, as we know, is unduly capricious. But an act of grace can easily make it sing to the Guru’s tune. Attunement to the Guru gets the mind in line, allowing the ideas of a higher consciousness to seep through.

These ideas are but the elements of Truth, radical for the intellect entrenched in short- termism yet as ancient as the universe to those who have stuck to dharma. The Guru shows us through the experiences we undergo that there is dharma, righteousness, truth, God. Once our learned scepticism is eroded, he shows how dharma and faith work in our lives, how they provide sanctuary and safe harbour and how they free the life encased in a human body into the great ocean of being. Through the Guru’s tending, the aspirant feels a natural expansion of his feelings and thoughts. He loses self-worry and gains concern for others. Not through something as blunt and obvious as moralizing sermons, but through a keen method known as the Guru which opens his eyes.

Yet one man’s experiences cannot be another man’s fodder. All the learning that can be expected from another’s experience is like akin to reading a book in a language not one’s own. The experiences of humankind have to be distilled into the disciple till he feels one with mankind’s sorrows and joys and realizes that his destiny is that of the human race. That his singular liberation is not just for him, but that in it lies the light that shines for all.

The Guru’s task is to make the disciple, understandably reluctant because this is not the romantic spiritual holiday he may have expected, undergo training. How the Guru does this he only knows, but he uses the pattern of the disciple’s life to train him, to make him spiritually fit. This is the most wondrous aspect of the Guru. It is not the supernatural powers he possesses, his intense physical presence that constantly bursts with the divine energy behind it, but the wholesome yet inscrutable way in which he prepares the disciple that is amazing. Too much attention is at times paid to fitness tests for the Guru, but as the Vedic saying has it: Both the Guru and the disciple have to be ideal. They have to fit like a hand in a glove.

The Guru is an impulse given to the being within you to out itself. He is the igniter of the spark, and no spiritual combustion can take place, no spiritual engine can fire, without the true spark of the Guru. A false guru just can’t start the ignition. The Guru does not bring spirituality from the outside; he shows it within you. The Guru’s job is not of transportation of learning, mystical, ritualistic or secular, but of transcending the ignorance of the disciple. He takes a pickaxe to your ignorance and destroys it forever. “The first lamp is the Guru,” says Swami Vivekananda, “and the lamp that is lighted from it is the disciple.”

Before that aspiration is reached, preparatory steps are called for1. First, the disciple must want the Guru. He must develop love for him, for then alone can he trust him. As the novice begins to stir with devotion, he begins to feel inadequate to this love. This is very natural. If you love a superior person, you soon see where you are coming up short. It is not a reaction prodded by the person of the Guru, but a realization brought about by love itself.

Second, the disciple must want to become worthy of the Guru’s love. He acknowledges, without the guidance of moral strictures, his misalignment from what he sees in the Guru.

Third, he must make the effort to purify himself. To rise in love, as the common saying has it. This purification may be brought about by prayers, rituals, mantras, and other spiritual exercises, but it is not necessary that it should be. A glance of grace from the Guru can set in motion internal processes that bring out and cleanse states of being. It is not a purification that is ceremonial, but a cleansing of the mental swamp that is our common store of mischief. It can come about through sadhana and sewa. (The karmas that make up the body must be exhausted in service—that is sewa. The devotee thinks he is doing selfless service at the behest of the Guru, but it is actually the Guru that is taking pains on one’s behalf. Sewa is the wish-fulfilling tree and provides refuge).

Fourth, he must drop the ego. He must cleave less to outer circumstance to fortify himself and depend fully on the Guru. He views himself less as an individual and more as an agency. His commitment to self-seeking and self-aggrandising goals wanes as he feels their hollow transitoriness and acknowledges their futility. He ropes his ego to something more gratifying, more fulfilling. Yet the nature of the higher good is such that the ego can’t latch on to it. The devotee finds that the dharma cannot feed his ego, and the Guru absolutely not. The effect is that he develops emotional maturity. He may love and yearn for the Guru as much as he wants, but these emotions become interiorized. His prayers may continue, but they lose sentimentality and self-pity. The devotee gives up all self-seeking, he does not seek to bend circumstances towards personal gain. Instead, the disciple sees the hand of the Guru in the shaping of circumstances.

Fifth, he surrenders to the Guru. Coeval with the relinquishment of the ego comes the Guru’s gift of surrender. It is said that the disciple must surrender to the Guru. But who is going to surrender to whom? How can surrender be willed? The minute it is done through force of mind or even through an act of understanding it becomes not surrender, but the effect of an action ordained by an individual. A subtle form of the ego hides behind such noble and meritorious acts, for the true cause of surrender is the Guru’s grace 2.

Sixth, he becomes one with the Guru. As such this not a step, but a consequence of all that came before. The effect of the Guru’s grace is tivra or ugra, rapid, after the ego is gone and just the remnants of the personality remain. The personality is then like a shell within which the Guru has his abode. The disciple and the Guru are one. The Guru works through the disciple, who has lost his sense of wrongful identity and wholly knows who he is, to take the disciple towards the realization, as Shri Ramana Maharishi put it, that “God, Guru and Self are one.” The spiritual light leaps from one bodily frame to another and passes on, ever-lit. The Guru and disciple, thus, form an informal tradition of a continuous ‘showing of lights’, or arati, to the divine that is present in everyone even as it is beyond and above them.

Seventh, divine sport. The way once walked assiduously has been transcended. The heroism of the soul has realized its aspiration and been consumed by it. Now, what happens is leela. The divine plays through you to bring others to the divine threshold. A fully realized soul, who has transcended all limitations, now is godly, is holy, is sacred even if and when enmeshed in the barbarism that the flesh is heir to.

Knowledge, power, purification, worship, doership all these have been bid goodbye. The soul is done. Even God is done. There is nothing to attain, nothing to fear and nothing to hope for. No beginnings and no ends, for all beings and ends are in you. Being and non- being are the two hands of this divine personage whose foot is on the Earth and whose head in the highest heavens. Through his pores he sustains the universe and through his formlessness he holds it together and goes beyond it. This is not liberation and realization, but the transcending flight of the self beyond the causal dream of the world into the infinite real.

And the ideal book on the Guru that isn’t in the works, while Grace is

Books that start with questions are not go-tos for most people. My question in beginning to write this is: What kind of book would I like to read about Guruji and about spirituality? The first answer is that I would love to have in my hand a large, extremely well-written book about the Guru-disciple relationship as seen through the eyes of the disciple, as witnessed in his life, and as expressed in the soulful intimacy of the tie. On top of it, I would like to get the Guru’s perspective, too. (Perspective because that is what we are within the limits of language calling it.) The last could become available if the Guru chanced to have mercy on the poor guy undertaking this building of words where the relationship could be housed, and if the writer became a channel of transmission. The only way to get it would be to pray, beseech, plead, throw tantrums, and then settle down before a white page of uncertainty.

The Guru’s perspective on the disciple and the spiritual affair would have to be a transmission. The narration of the biographical aspects—how the contact was made, the moment of binding, the introduction to the teachings, the playing-out of the path in real life, the learnings, the movement ahead—and the forging of the relationship: how it was tended to by the Guru, how it was used to teach the disciple, how it became a conduit of love and protection, how it showed the Guru to be what he or she was—all this would be a matter of retrospective reckoning for the disciple.

Finally, all of this should fit under that great umbrella of ‘spirituality’. Which doesn’t mean a grand finale that led to some halo around the disciple’s neck or the bestowal of some special powers. (It would decent if these were conferred one would like to think. We could, for starters, escape the common cold for the rest of our lives and, further along, have certifiable moments of absolutely blinding bliss and incorruptible intuition.)

But spirituality isn’t wishful thinking or wishful getting. It is how we spend our lives, and how it fits into our lives. To that end one too has to show the hand of the Guru, Guruji, in shaping lives.

At the outset then, one asks for benediction and grace from His Majesty, his beautiful caring and unfailing love that has swooped down into the miasma and mess of my life (as it was, as it was) and cleaned it up, made its ground softer and more pliable, more accommodating to the seeds of steadfast virtue and humble yet strong growth that he has so laboured to farm. I also put my palms out, open, longing, for grace and wisdom and art in the craft of writing and telling and bringing you, devotees and readers, into the space where the Guru and disciple meet. This place is not a location, and it is not meant to be for just a season, it is there for all time and for all the roles we have to play.

One should never fail to mention that though writing is undoubtedly creative, revising and getting it just right is where genius lies. Since I am poorly possessed of both, I must again entreat the Creator, Shiva Himself, to cast a benign look at this endeavour even as I realize what a folly it is to ask Him to do so. Here he is engaged in the five-fold aspects or acts of His being: creation, sustenance, reabsorption, concealment, and grace. And here I am with pen in hand, hoping to shine a light on how His grace in the form of His becoming my Guru enlightened my life. It is foolishness, but since anugraha is an aspect of his being, I might as well press on. For the Lord is reputed to help the foolish far more than the wise who can take care of themselves. He is Bholenath and my protector.

But you, fellow travellers, I pray, you too cast a gentle eye on this travelogue. May you treat errant words and arrant remarks with forgiveness, thinking kindly that he is but a knave, and his words are an entertaining repast, a light breath of frivolity, a mere undercurrent in the high seriousness of our endeavour. For though some would have it that the spiritual journey is a serious matter, to my mind it is more sincerity than talk and work, and if I fail to impress you in these, please put it down to the trespass of the fool.

It would be nice to assure you with my qualifications at this point in time. Indeed, the entire matter of being a spiritual Adhikari is undeniable. You have to be capable of what you want. And yet capacity alone isn’t what the Guru sees, I believe. I have to believe, otherwise I’d have no legs left to stand upon. There is the ability to hold and give out love, to be unbiased, to be fully human, to be spiritually sensitive, to be willing to be tested, to be willing to walk on when certain disappointment looms ahead and distrust keeps nipping at your feet. I believe this is all about Trust and Love (the ones that are adorned with capitals in the bazaar of values). And you could equally translate these as Faith. And what is Faith but knowing God’s will? And what else is certain of God’s wish but that it is unknowable. For to know God is to partake of His nature by His grace or, as they, say: To know god is to be god (even if momentarily, in small caps—in fact, to know the G-O-D is to go beyond being an embodied person). Knowing God’s will is a huge presumption. Yet, devotees of all times and climates, have known it, have borne witness to its workings and sometimes given an account of it. Autobiography of a Yogi—yes? The surrendered songs of Mira Bai, the imperious dohas of Kabir, the transcendental power and immense sweetness of the Gurubani. Though foolishly conjoined together, these are the words of God or, more accurately, His word made familiar in human speech by spiritual giants.

Where do I stand? What can I tell you that would add to this even a comma? But it is Guruji’s love that comes to the fore and makes each one of us unique. The shade of the tree has its use as does the peak of Everest. The Himalayas change the path of the monsoons and, thus, of civilizational history while the tree in its dogged rootedness, rests a pilgrim and nests a brood. I am not a tree. I am a leaf playing a minor melody before it touches the ground of its being. I am rustling words and you are listening from the zone of your large, capacious being, allowing these to be part of your soundscape, part of your journey.

The Guru, of course, is the sky. There might not be an ‘of course’ about it for some. The Guru might be seen as a human being, a personality harbouring likes and dislikes, favouring this over that, possessed by moods, thoughts, feelings and fancy, whimsical in making decisions, giving orders, bowing to the ebb and flow of life as we do, succumbing to illness and rumour and undignified opinion, rising above temptation and greed, nobly giving, sacrificing: in a word, being completely human. That, to my mind, is the great trick of the Sadguru. That he is human. That you may see him from the vantage of human eyes. But the contents of the sky are not the sky and though the contents may inspire awe and wonder and homage, the sky is untouched by homily or censure. Its vantage point, its point of view, the Guru’s perspective is Godlike. The Guru does indisputably and fully know God, and God knows the Guru: My Father and I are one only then. To be less than this is not to be the Sadguru even though one may be incredibly spiritually felicitous. The Sadguru is really God. You may be incredulous of this truism, because of the dress of humanity that the Guru wears. Yet this is a dress; it is not, as we would say, the person himself.

I am the leaf and he is the sky. And this relationship, both biographical and spiritual, is what I would like to read a book about. That, as I suggested above, is the first kind of book I’d like to read.

The second kind book of I’d have is a treatise of sorts on the teachings of Guruji. An elegantly crafted manual, sutra-like in brevity, with a clear exposition of the teaching. Like this:

First sutra: To revere your Guru is to love him.

Elaboration: Revere means to hold in abiding love. To abide in love means to find no change in affection regardless of circumstance. To hold in affection means to be firm in faith. Hence, being firm in faith is abiding in love.

Second sutra: The Guru is the Guru always.

Elaboration: After you have tested and welcomed your Guru, after you have affirmed to your satisfaction that he is you guru fully, you are always his disciple no matter your attainment or lack of it. In the farce, tragedy and utter humanity of this world-stage, the Guru appears in many guises and appears to wear many faces. He could be a silent mauni, he could be mirthful, he could be powerful, he could be as tender as the mother, as severe as a disciplinarian, he could be severely demanding on you even as he gives ample latitude to the rest, he could test you even as he lets others do as they wish, and far worse, he could willfully and fully ignore you. He is in all the outward moods of his fulfilled and perfect being, your guru. Your obeisance must never falter; there must be an inner sacredness of approach towards him.

Third sutra: The disciple is the melody the Guru creates. Elaboration: The Guru’s job is to make you like him. Your resistance is your perversity. But your resistance is a fact of your being, you cannot dismantle it in one go. So let the Guru work on it. You try to be less resistant, more pliable, more accepting. The Guru, in this sense, can be what life brings you. As you accommodate yourself to the vicissitudes of life, the Guru supports you, teaches you and keeps you from falling apart. Since the Guru wants you to be perfected, wants you to reach the single root of your being—which is indistinguishable from life—you are like a violin to his bow. He keeps playing till he hears the note that signifies you have lost yourself in the music that He is.

Fourth sutra: The Guru’s Word is law.

To revere the Guru, to abide in Love, to inhere in faith, to have a sacred synchronicity with him – all these require that the disciple abides and respects and acts in accordance with the Guru’s injunctions. This shows to the Guru that he is taken seriously and the disciple is as interested in fulfilling the aims of the Guru-disciple relationship as the Lord who is the Guru himself. All the injunctions of the Guru, related to either the practical world or to the invisible repository of mercy he represents, are meant to benefit the disciple. They are blessings in disguise meant to protect, teach, and enlighten a disciple. A Sadguru’s words are direct commands to the world of nature itself. The disciple who aligns himself with these stands the only sure chance of winning His grace.

Fifth sutra: The Guru never lets go of the disciple.

This spiritual tie is irrevocable. A Guru is not a person alone; he is reality made manifest. So when he sheds his mortal form, the disciple is not left wanting. The relationship is not abrogated with the fact of the Guru’s physical death. The Guru’s spiritual reality comes to the fore and through the invisible soul-strings he intervenes in his disciple’s life, teasing out his karma and preparing him for the path. Reality is not dependent on form. The vehicle of grace takes many shapes in conformity with the disciple’s need for security, love, and abidance.

Sixth sutra: Guru is Being Expressing Love

The Guru is the source and expression of love. It is life reaching out to you with open arms, not literally, not smothering you with sentiment, but walking with you when you are alone. And when are you not alone? Kid yourself not. All that you take to be ‘my’ and ‘mine’ is maya. As Sant Tulsidas writes in Ramacharitmanas:

“Me and mine, you, and yours—this is maya, which has captivated all jivas. The senses and their objects, and that up to which the mind can reach, O brother, know all that to be maya.”

The mind itself, just a stream of water flowing outward, wavelets coming up as the waters from the incoming impulses goes over the riverbed of samskaras, or past impressions: Ingrained responses, trigger points, behavioural tendencies, emotional and irrational habits, oohs and aahs, wows and violences. That mind conjured up by samskaras interposes itself as an apparent personality, an identity, an interloper that makes an image in contradistinction to the limpid Being of the Guru. That has to be put in abeyance, relinquished, abandoned—and the Guru’s victory fully realized in reclaiming one’s true identity.

Seventh Sutra: The method of the Guru

For the ever-victorious Guru then the task is to shatter the image. How may this be done? Few in the history of humanity have achieved by themselves because you need an outer to point to the inner. To say, Hey Shvetaketu, You are That! The Guru, thus, comes for the fortuitous few who hearken to his call and subtly, urgently, insistently points to the centre of the disciple’s self. He reorients the disciple. He shows that Existence works on its own, for its own and through its own means. That no one is excluded. In fact, there is neither inclusion nor exclusion. But the best way to partake of such open-ended blessings is to quietly be. And the method of the Guru changes naturally, instinctively as the shape of the disciple changes before him. Whatever needs to pare the ego, to wear the personality down is offered by the Guru as karma comes to the disciple. The Guru points to a path that eventually, perfectly, naturally shows the disciple who he is. This can be devotion, wisdom, service, yoga, or none of these. It may just be meeting the Guru physically every day. For that energy can and does transform the disciple. Not into a superman or super mind, but into a very human, sweet, loving being that demands nothing, desires nothing, and abides simply naturally in whatever circumstances bring him.

Thus, circumstances might bring about a book different to the one you wish for. The book I am desperate for is the book that opens the key to the Guru’s heart, that provides a clear, follow-able path that is certain to hit the goal of my life. I do not care if it is arcane: I could like it. But it must be a genuine teaching that is transmitted to me. I do not worry if it is outside the tradition that is being followed by a majority of devotees, it just should not be proscribed by my Guru. I am not sure if such a book exists. I am quite sure spirituality is not a gym-plan that one can train on. (But who knows!) I am sure transmissions exist, even in the form of books, and only need a consummate teacher to prize them open. It is a perfected disciple that can draft this book; I can only wait for it.
