Caesura

Silence is the ground of creativity. But it gets lost in the ushering noise of ill- formed thoughts. Strands of thoughts spill over each other in unshapely inkpools, text bloats, punctuation swirls, and the thread of the narrative is lost.

Editing reclaims the primacy of meaning. It restores order, establishes sense, harmonises form and function, ascertains context and foregrounds creativity. The best of editing is an invisible affirmation of the writer’s voice, an unseen marking of her thought in the background of the page. It is applied forethought; it is the application of just the right space to the right amount of words. For: It is in the silence between words that meaning is held together.

And, at times, it needs another pair of eyes and hands.

Crosscurrents

The Responsibility of Rhetoric to a Meadow

Is restraint actually a responsibility in times of barbarism? Confronted with terror, aren’t we allowed to rant? Doesn’t the helplessness of the bystander or the outrage of the violated victim in the face of a body blow to the dharma of humanity and its entire social ethos need to be vented?﻿Assuredly so. Expression is a function of speech and of civilizational discourse. But silence is our felt response when horror turns its face upon us—or when holiness does so too. We instinctively know that terror that overwhelms all its murderous precedents can’t be voiced against or over. And we know that what is uniquely and forever holy when seen against the class of all precedents cannot be glorified. We can only respond to it with silent awe. Note Arjun’s disorientation when faced with the glory of Krishna. Note our silent tears when we were efficiently, cooly, and cockily killed in a peaceful meadow in Kashmir. We were killed because those who were murdered were our extended selves. They were killed because they represented a free and happy humanity. Bestial animalistic barbarity and supra-human glory, both, rob human beings of the bandwidth of perceptual responses. We struggle to emote against or for. The holy invites prayer and supplication, while the subhuman savage discredits all ideas of communication, and then invites a rhetoric of retribution. Our wounds gape silently; our words catch up later.

However, given the propensity of our minds to be swayed into intemperate discourse (internal and external), there are boundaries, if you will—placed even upon prayer (which is but an extollation of the holy, a stuti, after we have experienced the sacred briefly) as a public medium. Sant Kabirji says sharply: “Kankar pathar jori ke masjid lai chunai / Ta chadhi mulla baang de kya bahira hua Khudai?” A mosque is built with stones and pebbles / A cleric crows from its minaret: Is God hard of hearing? But what about the other case: When humanity itself requires redemptive justice? Should there be hedges on our speech even there? Yes. Allowances have to be made for the space in which violated emotions can be expressed. But once terror has created a theatre of barbarism, we need to ensure we do not enter it again—not via words, because that is easily done. Words ignite powerful emotions. And, when barbarism has been especially designed to provoke, we need to stay clear of its theatre and allow time for a response. Not a reaction, because that can play into the anti-human, anti-civilizational design that defines the sub-human barbaric that vented its bestial savagery for a quarter or a third of an hour and stained a green meadow red. Let us posit this differently. Shall we respond to the sharks that hunt and naturally consider blood food by baying for their blood in turn? The responsibility of rhetoric lies in allowing time and space to those who exercise power in our stead. Rhetoric has to point out that those who were killed were denuded of their human identity. Rhetoric has to not let the arms of power forget that this was a crime against humanity and a violation of unitary social ties, such as that of family, marriage, childhood, just bonding. Rhetoric’s responsibility is to push the arms of power and the deliberation of justice towards a punishment that clearly avenges all of humanity. In fact, rhetoric needs to remind justice if it gets too punctilious with its laws.

Finally, rhetoric needs to prod justice to make allowance for a space of peace. That allows those were fatally outraged to find the other shore for their spirit, a nobler designation, a calm sanctuary that lets them walk away from the red bloodlust of a once-alive meadow. If rhetoric is overwhelmed by calls for dead bodies, a fully fervent war, and for waters to flow incarnadine, it runs the real risk of operationalizing the powerful engine of the war machine before it is oiled, positioned, provisioned for, and defended. That in turn serves against the raison d’être of the rhetoric: Justice. And an avengeful one at that, a ferocious attack whose splinters lodge themselves in the military body of the enemy now and forever in its racial memory. Since deterrence doesn’t work, war has to. But for war to work, victory has to be assured and, more difficult, swift.﻿In this context, rhetoric has to fight at the subtle frontiers of narrative-building. It has to find symbols and images that emphasize the inhumanity and irreligiosity, the lack of reverence for the sacred and unconcealed disgust for the humane that characterise subhuman barbarism. It has to reawaken the myths that speak of the subterranean urges, the dark wells and seductive veils of evil, and expose the psyche that lies behind terror. It can paint the enemy with the palette that terror chose or reveal its true face, by contrast, via stoic humanism. What it must not allow for is the enemy to wear the emotional patina of victimhood and the mask of historical, geographical, or imagined injury. It has to forbid terror from justifying any equipment of violence as a moral defence. And it has to do this as the narrative forms with punches and counterpunches, calling for explanations, examination, inquiry, guilt-shaming, legal swordplay, diplomatic fencing, and exculpation. Rhetoric has to be responsible even as we search for the enemies within—rightly—and without, but not one hopes blindly.

Humans are not survivalists alone. They belong together.﻿ This terror and those who have called it out of its den aren’t human. Terrorism isn’t inspired by courage. Or ideas. Its subhuman and inchoate self is seduced by the age-old promise of lasciviousness, here or hereafter. It is primed by savagery, the seeds of its finer instincts never nurtured. Terror itself comes from a primeval and base fear, that of survival in a jungle. It is not designed for coexistence; it is structured to be unfit for compassion. It does not seek solace or succour; it places these in a belief system where zombies live in a post-carnage paradise. What a contemplation. Humanity confronts terror with courage, because it is not only primed to survive (as evolutionist will have it), but also designed to be sociable and loved. Thus, it finds its courage against the subhuman, it avenges and claims itself, and finally it pities the darkness that births the den of terror. For the darkness cannot be helped. It is beyond redemption. Even its idea of heaven is based on the creation of hell.

The outrage of our voices can find rest only when justice has dignified humanity again, thunderously punished those who violated it. Give justice some time to simmer and brood so that it can place its sure hands on the neck of evil and not merely wrestle with it. Nor should it find only fleeting shadows to fight against—which will fail the entire enterprise and bring the ordinary bystander back to his helpless self and deprive the grief-stricken of resolution. There is a meadow in a valley that needs to be made green, bright, and human again. Then alone can those who were denied it be made whole again and bid adieu. Om Shanti Shanti Shanti
